Executive Summary

California faces a worsening wildfire crisis. This project delivers a county-level severe wildfire forecast for 2016–2026, built on 189,550 historical fire records (1992–2015) from the US Forest Service Fire Program Analysis Fire-Occurrence Database (FPA-FOD).

We aggregate raw fire records into a county × season × year panel (2,881 rows, 59 counties) and train three models — Poisson Regression, Random Forest, and Gradient Boosting — to predict the count of severe fires (≥300 acres) per county-season. Features include temporal lag patterns, cumulative historical severity rates, fire volume, and ENSO climate signals (El Niño/La Niña) to capture fuel-loading dynamics. Gradient Boosting achieves the best validation MAE of 0.418.

Forecasts are rolled forward year-by-year through 2026, anchored to published NOAA ENSO conditions. Results are distilled into a county risk ranking to guide pre-season resource allocation, and cross-validated against an independent fire-level Random Forest classifier (AUC = 0.9994). An interactive map with a year slider and seasonal filters provides an exploration interface for emergency management decision-makers.

Problem Statement

California's wildfire seasons have grown dramatically in severity. Emergency managers must make pre-season decisions — where to pre-position crews, how to allocate aerial suppression resources, which counties need mutual-aid agreements — months before fires occur. These decisions require forward-looking county-level risk estimates, not retrospective analysis.

Existing tools focus on real-time fire behavior once ignition occurs. Our project addresses the planning horizon problem: given historical fire patterns and climate conditions, which California counties are forecast to experience the most severe wildfire activity (fires ≥300 acres) in the coming year, and how should limited resources be allocated across a 2016–2026 horizon?

Severe fire threshold: 300 acres. A fire of this size requires multi-agency response, aerial support, and potential evacuation orders. Predicting severe fire counts — not just whether any fire occurs — directly maps to resource scaling decisions.

2 — Methodology

Panel Aggregation

We transform 189,550 individual fire records into a county × season × year panel of 2,881 rows. Each row represents one county–season–year combination and records: total fire count, severe fire count, mean lat/lon centroid, and engineered features. This aggregation converts an individual event classification problem into a count forecasting problem — more appropriate for resource planning.

We use a strict temporal split — no data from future years leaks into training — simulating a genuine forecasting scenario. The model is trained once on 1997–2012, validated on 2013–2015, then rolled forward.

ENSO integration (Jacob's contribution): El Niño years bring above-normal rainfall to California → dense grass and shrub growth → elevated fuel load that dries and burns 1–2 years later. By encoding 1-year and 2-year lagged ENSO scores, the model captures this delayed mechanism. ENSO data is sourced from NOAA/CPC and extended through 2026, allowing genuine forward-looking forecasts anchored to published climate outlooks.

4 — Forecast 2016–2026

Figure 7: Total predicted severe fires across all counties and seasons, 2016–2026

Forecasts are generated by rolling the model forward year-by-year: each year's predicted severe count becomes the lag feature for the following year. This preserves the temporal dependency structure without data leakage.

Unlike a flat extrapolation, the forecast shows climate-driven year-to-year variation driven by ENSO conditions:

  • 2016 (112 fires): La Niña follows the strong 2015 El Niño — fuel accumulated in wet years dries rapidly
  • 2017 (78 fires): Weak La Niña, below-average prediction
  • 2020–2021 (118/108 fires): Moderate La Niña sequence amplifies fire risk
  • 2022 (77 fires): Strong La Niña — suppresses near-term ignition activity
  • 2023 (101 fires): Strong El Niño rebound — vegetation growth signal
Methodology note: Fire volume for forecast years is set to the 2013–2015 county-season average (the last known period). ENSO scores for 2016–2025 use published NOAA values; 2026 uses the neutral assumption. Forecasts represent expected counts under historical fire frequency conditions.

6 — Limitations & Next Steps

Limitations

  • No drought index (PDSI/NDVI) — ENSO captures inter-annual cycles but not local soil moisture conditions
  • Training data ends 2015 — extreme recent seasons (2017 Wine Country, 2018 Camp Fire, 2020–2021 mega-fires) are not reflected in the model
  • Fire volume for forecast years is fixed at 2013–2015 average — does not account for potential increases in ignition frequency under climate change
  • County centroids approximated from fire location averages, not official administrative boundaries
  • ENSO for 2026 uses a neutral assumption — forecast uncertainty increases at the outer horizon

Next Steps

  • Incorporate PDSI Palmer Drought Severity Index as a monthly county-level covariate
  • Retrain on post-2015 data to capture the recent mega-fire regime shift
  • Add prediction intervals (bootstrap or quantile regression) around county-level forecasts
  • Integrate Rajesh's fire-size class pipeline for real-time dispatch recommendations alongside our planning-horizon forecasts
  • Deploy the interactive map as a hosted web application for emergency management agencies
